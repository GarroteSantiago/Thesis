# C40: Stock hardware good enough

## Definition
General-purpose processors, with better compilers, reach the performance that once justified special-purpose machines. Merges "stock hardware", "value lay in the software, not the hardware" and "general-purpose hardware good enough".

## First found in
P15

## What the papers say, compared
- **Lisp.** Stock hardware means "commercially available, general-purpose, 'off-the-shelf' computers" (P15, p. 23); "General-purpose hardware eventually became good enough to support Lisp once again", largely through fast RISC processors, and "the value lay ultimately in the software and not in its enabling hardware technology" (P15, pp. 15, 35).
- **Smalltalk.** A RISC with a few additions (SOAR) runs Smalltalk at about the speed of the Dorado (P11, p. 66); a specialised machine "must also exploit compilation to attain high performance" (P11, p. 17).
- **High-level-language machines.** Better compilers and cheaper memory removed their motivation (P280, pp. 98–99).
- **Capability kernels.** "capability systems can perform well without resorting to specialized hardware assists" (P77, p. 182).
- **Message machines, from their own designers.** The J-Machine's evaluators propose folding its mechanisms "into a conventional RISC processor core without affecting sequential performance" (P65, p. 12).
- **The other side.** Barrelfish's authors expect hardware may "drop [cache coherence] entirely in favor of a message-passing model" (P8, p. 5), and the SCC's designers argue coherence overhead will outgrow the value of more cores (P21, p. 173). Both are arguments, not measurements.
- **Comparison.** Every retrospective read agrees the stock-hardware argument won in the past; the only counter-arguments concern many-core chips and are unmeasured.
