# C14: Microkernels and multiserver operating systems

## Definition
A kernel reduced to address spaces, IPC and basic scheduling, with operating system services and drivers running as isolated user-level servers that communicate by messages. Merges "microkernel", "first-generation vs second-generation microkernels", "conservative and progressive criteria", "processor-dependent microkernel", "trusted computing base", "multiserver operating system", "multikernel vs microkernel" and "wheel of reincarnation".

## First found in
P46

## What the papers say, compared
- **Definition and its generations.** Liedtke defines the microkernel as "minimizing the kernel and implementing servers outside the kernel" (P46, p. 70). First-generation kernels "evolved step by step from monolithic kernels" and were "inefficient and inflexible"; the second generation is designed "from scratch" (P46, pp. 70, 74). He also holds that microkernels are "inherently not portable" (P46, p. 74) and sets two tests: applications must not be degraded, and new kinds must be supported (P46, pp. 72–73).
- **Why they were rejected, and why they might return.** Liedtke calls "the pure microkernel approach of the first generation ... a failure" for its measured penalty (P46, pp. 73, 77). Tanenbaum et al. agree they were "long discarded as unacceptable because of their lower performance", and see a comeback for reliability: "The wheel of reincarnation has turned" (P49, pp. 47, 50). They differ on the cause: Liedtke blames the first generation's design, while Minix 3's designers call a small loss (under 10% for user-mode drivers) inherent (P49, p. 47).
- **Durability.** The minimality principle is "Retained" after 20 years of L4 (P48, p. 136).
- **A multicore objection.** Barrelfish's authors say a microkernel "remains a shared-memory, multithreaded system in the kernel", which their multikernel avoids (P8, p. 17; see C16).
