# C41: Adoption factors: compatibility, market and price

## Definition
Reasons other than raw performance why a machine or operating system is or is not adopted. Merges "cachet", "dedicated computers", "AI winter" and "compatibility as reason for coherence".

## First found in
P15

## What the papers say, compared
- **Market and marketing (third parties on the 432).** It "tried to do too many new things all at once", and its market "may still not be large enough or defined well enough" (P10, p. 337); its failure was "probably due in part to premature (and somewhat overzealous) marketing" (P72, p. 186).
- **Price and dedicated machines.** Customers "were not willing to buy expensive 'dedicated' computers and then spend money maintaining them" (P15, p. 35); adoption was driven partly by "cachet", and the "AI winter" hurt Lisp companies (P15, pp. 15, 29–30). Smalltalk environments "suffer from poor cost-performance" (P11, p. 10) and "require such expensive computers that few programmers can afford them" (P13, abstract; abstract only).
- **Compatibility and existing investment.** Systems "tend not to abandon compatibility to eliminate small costs" (P22, p. 1); there is "enormous investment" in Linux and Windows, and a scalability problem "must affect a substantial group of users" before it gets attention (P8, pp. 1, 16). HLLCs supported other languages poorly (P280, pp. 98–99).
- **Designers' view of their own machine.** The MDP's designers name the lack of well-established parallel benchmarks and programs "biased by the mechanisms ... of the machines for which they were initially written" (P12, pp. 23–24).
- **Business.** KeyKOS development "ceased in 1991 when the company closed" (P77, p. 170).
- **Comparison.** Third parties stress market, price and compatibility; designers stress benchmarks and implementation. Compatibility (P22, P8) is the factor that applies unchanged to any new machine today.
