# P77

## Reference
Shapiro, J. S., Smith, J. M., Farber, D. J. (1999). *EROS: a fast capability system*. Proceedings of the 17th ACM Symposium on Operating Systems Principles (SOSP '99), Kiawah Island, SC, pp. 170–185. https://doi.org/10.1145/319151.319163. Included under I1 (kernel IPC by capability invocation, I1(c), p. 173–174).

## Deepest pass
3 (sections 3, 4 and 6 read in detail for the message mechanism, the non-message paths and the measurements; the related-work section read for the other systems it compares)

## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** Design, implementation and microbenchmark evaluation of a capability-based microkernel with a single-level persistent store (EROS) on a commodity processor, the Pentium (p. 170–171, 180).
- **Context:** The belief that "the desirable features of transparent persistence and capability-based protection are widely believed to have prohibitive performance", which the paper says "is largely justified by experience" with the i432, Mach and the AS/400 (p. 170–171). It builds on GNOSIS/KeyKOS (TymShare) as "a clean-room reimplementation of the GNOSIS architecture in C++" (p. 170), on L3/L4 IPC design (p. 181–182) and on the authors' earlier IPC and state-caching papers [44], [45] (p. 171, 173, 176).
- **Correctness:** Assumes that lmbench-inspired microbenchmarks with "semantically similar" EROS operations are a fair comparison with Linux 2.2.5 (p. 180); the paper itself states that "the microbenchmarks currently available for EROS do not support general conclusions about application performance" (p. 171) and that "A full validation of our performance claim requires application-level benchmarks" (p. 183). The claim that microbenchmarks "approximately predict real system performance" rests on KeyKOS experience [5, 16], not on EROS measurements (p. 183).
- **Contributions:** (1) The EROS kernel architecture: a few primitive capability types (numbers, nodes, pages, processes, entry and resume capabilities) and capability invocation as the only system call (p. 172–174); (2) software caching of nodes and pages into machine-specific forms (process table as a cache, page tables as a read-only cache, producer and product chains) (p. 175–178); (3) user-level storage allocation (space bank), virtual copy and process construction (p. 179–180); (4) microbenchmarks showing EROS "performs favorably on 6 out of 7 benchmarks" against Linux on the same machine (p. 182).
- **Clarity:** Well structured and readable. Figure 11 uses the large-to-small context switch (1.19 µs) as the EROS value, while the text gives 1.60 µs for the large-space case, which is slower than Linux's 1.26 µs (p. 181); the "6 out of 7" count (p. 182) depends on that choice (my observation; the paper does not discuss it).

## Concepts
| Concept | What the paper says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| capability invocation as the only system call | "capability invocation is the only 'system call' implemented by the kernel. Because there are no other system calls, all actions taken by a process are implicitly access checked." | p. 173–174 |
| capability | "an unforgeable pair made up of an object identifier and a set of authorized operations (an interface) on that object". | p. 171 |
| protection domain | "the set of capabilities accessible to a subsystem"; in EROS "each process is a protection domain". | p. 171, 173 |
| entry capability / resume capability | Entry capabilities let the holder invoke a process's services; a resume capability lets the recipient reply, and all its copies are consumed on use, "ensuring an 'at most once' reply". | p. 173 |
| transparent interposition | Because all capabilities take the same arguments, "processes implementing either mediation or request logging can be transparently interposed in front of most objects". | p. 174, 180 |
| access indirection / forwarding objects | A reference monitor supplies an indirection object or "inserts transparent forwarding objects in front of all capabilities that cross compartment boundaries"; revocation by destroying it. | p. 172, 174 |
| weak access right | Capabilities fetched via a weak capability are "both read-only and weak", ensuring "transitive read-only access". | p. 174 |
| single-level store / transparent persistence | "The single-level store's persistence is transparent to applications"; all state resides in pages and nodes. | p. 170, 175 |
| checkpoint with consistency check | Periodic snapshot of the entire machine, consistency-checked before commit; "Once committed, an inconsistent checkpoint lives forever". | p. 174 |
| the kernel owns no state | "With the sole exception of the list of stalled processes ... the EROS kernel owns no state." | p. 175 |
| software caching of objects | Pages and nodes cached at two levels; "Unlike conventional kernel architectures, this process table is a cache"; hardware mapping tables are a read-only cache of node trees. | p. 175–176, 178 |
| space bank (user-level storage allocation) | Application that "owns all system storage" and allocates nodes and pages; "uniquely as far as we know, user-level storage allocation". | p. 179, 183 |
| constructor | Trusted object that certifies whether a new process "has any ability to communicate with third parties", from its initial capabilities alone. | p. 180 |
| semantic gap | "The primitive abstractions implemented by the kernel map directly to the abstractions supported by the hardware. The 'semantic gap' between the two is minimal." | p. 183 |
| interrupt-style kernel | "When a process is suspended in the kernel it is forced to restart at the trap instruction; there is no per-process kernel stack." | p. 178 |
| small spaces | Segmentation-based address-space tagging after Liedtke, avoiding TLB flushes; "Small spaces have a disproportionate impact on the performance of an EROS system". | p. 177–178 |

## Notes
- **Source and version:** publisher's PDF (ACM SOSP '99 proceedings, pp. 170–185), downloaded from the Internet Archive's copy of https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/319151.319163 (direct ACM download returned 403) and saved as `research/papers/P77.pdf`. Text was extracted with pdftotext; Figure 11 was read from the rendered page image. An author preprint (PostScript, eros-os.org via the Internet Archive) exists but was not used.
- **Criterion:** I1(c) confirmed by the full text: IPC by capability invocation is the stated means by which processes, kernel services and user-level system services communicate, and "capability invocation is the only 'system call' implemented by the kernel" (p. 173–174). No exclusion criterion found. The paper cites the authors' earlier EROS papers on IPC [44] and state caching [45] (p. 185); if those enter the review, X8 should be checked (they report earlier results that this paper only cites).
- **For SQ6/SQ9 (outside I1's fields):** the paper states that "capability systems can perform well without resorting to specialized hardware assists" (p. 182); that the i432 "was designed as a high level language machine. Architecturally, it is a very complex machine, and its performance suffers dramatically as a result [7]" (p. 183); that the CAP computer's capability cache gave "adequate performance for large grain objects", but language-grain memory capabilities lead to "higher-frequency dereferencing of capabilities than the hardware can effectively support" (p. 182–183); and that the AS/400's performance "depends on an augmented processor instruction set and a tagged memory system" (p. 170–171).
- **Related message systems (p. 182):** Hydra's "port-based store and forward messaging system ... reduces its messaging performance"; CAL/TSS messaging "is unbuffered and portless", with an asynchronous bounded "event" mechanism; L3 IPC is unauthenticated, and access control by a "chief" process doubles "the cost of an authenticated IPC".
- **Baseline caveat:** the Linux page-fault baseline (687 µs) comes from a version the paper says had regressed (2.0.34: 67 µs) (p. 181); EROS (3.67 µs) is faster than both. The Figure 11 context-switch bar uses the large-to-small value (p. 181).
