## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** Position or analytical argument with a retrospective of first-generation microkernels (Mach, Chorus) and a short design description of second-generation ones (Exokernel, L4). Magazine article; its numbers are mostly measurements reported from other papers ([6], [8], [16]), not a new evaluation (pp. 70, 73–75).
- **Context:** First-generation microkernels Amoeba, Choices, Ra, V, Chorus, L3 and Mach (p. 71). Mach's external pager [22] (p. 71) and interrupts as IPC messages [17] (p. 72). Measurements by Chen and Bershad [6] and Condict et al. [8] (p. 73). Second-generation Exokernel [9] and L4 [16]; non-microkernel alternatives Spin [2], Synthesis [20], and loadable modules in Solaris and Linux (pp. 74–75).
- **Correctness:** The central claim is that first-generation inefficiency came from "the basic architecture of these kernels", not from inherent IPC cost, because "the hardware-inherited costs of mode and address-space switching are only 3%–7% of the measured costs" (p. 74). The argument rests on the Figure 4 breakdown and on the faster L4 and Exokernel numbers (pp. 74–75). The detailed evidence is deferred to [16] ("A detailed discussion can be found in [16]", p. 74). The author states that "There is no real-life practical experience with second-generation microkernels" (pp. 76–77).
- **Contributions:** A diagnosis of why first-generation microkernels were "inefficient and inflexible" (p. 70): IPC cost (pp. 72–74) and "the policy inside the microkernel" (p. 74). Two evaluation criteria, conservative and progressive (p. 72), and a relative-RPC-overhead model (Fig. 3, p. 73). A description of L4's recursive address spaces (grant, map, demap, with mapping done by IPC) and of the user-level memory servers, pagers, drivers and Unix server built on them (pp. 75–76).
- **Clarity:** Clear and well structured, with figures and FAQ sidebars (pp. 72, 76). The measurement setups are only summarised and depend on cited papers.

## Concepts
| Concept | What the paper says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| microkernel (new) | "minimizing the kernel and implementing servers outside the kernel. Ideally, the kernel implements only address spaces, interprocess communication (IPC), and basic scheduling." | p. 70 |
| first-generation vs second-generation microkernels (new) | The first generation "evolved step by step from monolithic kernels" and was "inefficient and inflexible". The second generation is designed "from scratch", with "a minimal and clean new architecture". | pp. 70, 74 |
| external pager (new) | "the kernel manages physical and virtual memory but forwards page faults to specific user-level tasks"; Mach's external pager was "the first conceptual breakthrough toward real microkernels". | p. 71 |
| interrupts as IPC messages (new) | "The kernel captures the interrupt but does not handle it, instead generating a message for the user-level process currently associated with the interrupt." | p. 72 |
| user-level device drivers (new) | Drivers run in user mode, are "encapsulated in address spaces", and can be "replaced, removed, or added dynamically". | p. 72 |
| conservative and progressive criteria (new) | Conservative: "Applications must not be degraded by the microkernel". Progressive: microkernels "must efficiently support new types of applications" that monolithic kernels cannot. | pp. 72–73 |
| RPC granularity / relative RPC overhead (new) | Overhead against "an ideal system where RPC is free", as a function of user-level cycles between RPCs. "Object-orientation and distribution will cause increased cross-address-space interaction", so "RPC granularity will become finer". | p. 73 |
| reintegration into the kernel (new) | Chorus "supervisor actors" and Mach "kernel-loaded tasks" "run in kernel mode, can freely access kernel space, and can freely interact with each other". This "weakens the microkernel approach". | p. 73 |
| policy inside the microkernel / mechanism, not policy (new) | "the weakness is the policy inside the microkernel". L4 "provides a pure mechanism interface", so "the kernel confines itself to mechanisms. Policies are left completely to user-level servers". | pp. 74–76 |
| processor-dependent microkernel (new) | "microkernels are inherently not portable, although they improve the portability of a whole system". The 486 and the Pentium need "different algorithms and data structures". | p. 74 |
| recursive address spaces: grant, map, demap (new) | Address spaces are built outside the kernel on top of an initial space holding physical memory. Grant and map require consent and are done by IPC; demap removes a page from all spaces that got it from the demapper. | p. 75 |
| protected control transfer (Exokernel) (new) | The "basic communication primitive" of Exokernel; it "crosses address spaces but does not transfer arguments". | p. 74 |
| trusted computing base (new) | "the set of all components whose correct functionality is a precondition for security"; in a microkernel system, hardware, microkernel, disk driver and perhaps a basic file system. | p. 71 |
