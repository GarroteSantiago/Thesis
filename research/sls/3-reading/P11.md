## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** Design and evaluation of a processor architecture and its run-time software, SOAR ("Smalltalk On A RISC"). It also contains a survey of earlier high-level-language machines (Ch. 2) and a new garbage collector, Generation Scavenging (Ch. 5) (pp. 1–5).
- **Context:** Smalltalk-80 runs poorly on widely available hardware. The only machine that "has demonstrated universally acceptable Smalltalk-80 performance" is the $120,000 ECL Xerox Dorado (p. 11). Earlier language machines (R-2, Burroughs B5700/B6700, Scheme-79, Symbolics 3600, Katana-32) "closely coupled the source language semantics to the hardware" (p. 14). RISC work (IBM 801, RISC I/II, MIPS) and the Deutsch-Schiffman software techniques for the 68010 are the other context (pp. 12–14, 24–28).
- **Correctness:** The premise is that a RISC with a few Smalltalk-specific additions can match microcoded or high-level-instruction machines. It is tested by instruction-level simulation of Xerox's official benchmarks (pp. 61–66). The NMOS chip exists and ran diagnostics, but Smalltalk-80 had not yet run on it (pp. 2, 127). The value of each feature is an omission cost measured in cycles under the current compiler. The author says this neglects cycle-time effects and compiler interaction (pp. 64–65, 67, 72).
- **Contributions:** (1) The SOAR instruction set, with tagged integer arithmetic, register windows, in-line call-target caching, fast shuffle, trap mechanisms and direct object pointers (Ch. 3). (2) A feature-by-feature evaluation that sorts features into "winners", "questionable" and "losers" (Tables 4.4–4.5, pp. 68–69). (3) Generation Scavenging, which reclaims storage with about 3% CPU overhead and no object table (Ch. 5, p. 126). (4) The "architect's trap" lesson (p. 70).
- **Clarity:** Clearly written and organised, with summary tables per chapter. The scan is legible but OCR-noisy, and a few table cells were checked against page images (pp. 66, 71).

## Notes
- **Source read:** `research/papers/P11.pdf` (249-page image scan; Acrobat image conversion, 2002). It has no text layer, so I OCR'd all pages with tesseract 5.5.2 at 200 dpi and checked key table pages (pp. 66, 71, 127, cover) against the page images. Version: the UC Berkeley dissertation, February 1986. The front matter includes a technical-report-style abstract page with DARPA contract numbers (PDF p. 1). This is not the MIT Press 1987 edition.
- **Chapters read:** front matter and abstract; Ch. 1 (pp. 1–5); Ch. 2 (pp. 6–29); Ch. 3 (pp. 30–60); Ch. 4 (pp. 61–73); Ch. 5 §5.1–5.2, §5.9.3–5.11 (pp. 74–75, 101–110); Ch. 7 (pp. 125–129); Appendix A §A.4.4–A.4.5 (pp. 185–192). Not read: Ch. 5 §5.3–5.9.2 (algorithm survey and detailed GS evaluation), Ch. 6 (medium-lifetime objects, analytical), and the rest of Appendices A and B (raw data).
- **Criterion:** the full text supports I2 as screened. I4 (follow-up pass, gate 3c 2026-10-13): SQ9 (pp. 2, 14, 24, 34–35, 125–126). See the follow-up bullet below. SOAR is described as an architecture for the Smalltalk-80 language, with simulated performance and stated reasons (pp. 2, 61–73). It does not meet I1: SOAR has no send or message instruction or mechanism (Table 3.2 p. 37), and sends are compiled to `call` plus software lookup (pp. 46–47). No verdict change proposed.
- **Discrepancy (Q6):** the abstract (PDF p. 8) says the language-specific hardware "doubles its performance over a RISC II with the same cycle time". Table 4.6 and p. 71 give a ratio of 1.6. Elsewhere the paper says the language-specific features "approximately double performance" (p. 4), and that register windows and tagged integers "nearly double SOAR's performance" (p. 125). These may measure different things. I record them as stated.
- **Evidence status:** most performance figures are cycle counts from simulation multiplied by 400 ns. The chips' effective cycle is 510 ns, and the chip ran diagnostics at 800 ns actual system cycle time (Table 3.8 p. 59; p. 127). So the "as fast as a Dorado" claim is a prediction: "the 400 ns NMOS SOAR chips that have been fabricated should run just as fast" (p. 73).
- **Process model:** process switches are deferred to safe calls and jumps through a software interrupt. All Smalltalk processes share one address space (pp. 45–46). This is relevant to SQ2 as a non-message, shared-memory base beneath the object model.
- **Follow-up pass (2026-10-13):** I read SQ1–SQ19 and re-OCR'd the scan (tesseract 5.5.2, 200 dpi), reading again pp. 1–29, 34–35, 50–51, 68–73, 80, 85, 102–105, 125–129 and the abstract (PDF pp. 7–8).
