# P22

## Reference
Milo M. K. Martin, Mark D. Hill, Daniel J. Sorin (2012). *Why On-Chip Cache Coherence Is Here to Stay*. Communications of the ACM 55(7), pp. 78–89. https://doi.org/10.1145/2209249.2209269. Included under I4 (SQ14), row R0-025.

All pages below are pages 1–9 of the authors' near-final version (`papers/P22.pdf`), not the CACM pages 78–89 (see Notes).

## Deepest pass
3. I read every section, the sidebar, Table 1 and Figures 2, 4 and 5, and checked the analyses (amortized traffic, storage growth, latency formulas, non-inclusive overhead) against the paper's own numbers. I did not rerun the recall simulation, because its inputs are not published.

## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** A position paper backed by analysis. It presents a proof-of-concept coherence design built from known techniques, then analyses it with analytical models and one small simulation. It reports no silicon or benchmark measurements (p. 1, p. 8).
- **Context:** It answers predictions that on-chip coherence will not scale and will give way to software-managed coherence, scratchpads or message passing without shared memory. It cites DeNovo [5], Intel's 48-core message-passing processor [10] and Cohesion [13] (p. 1, p. 3). It builds on directory protocols [1, 8], the SGI Origin 2000 [14], Intel Core i7 [17], AMD HT-Assist [6] and Linux scalability studies [2, 3] (p. 1, pp. 4–6).
- **Correctness:** Some assumptions are stated: coherence does not change the number of misses, though false sharing and poor synchronization may change it (p. 4). Others are stated too: an inclusive shared cache (p. 2), a single chip (p. 9), and no queuing or contention modelled (p. 8). Some are left implicit: the cost model counts bytes per miss and ignores how invalidation bursts are spread over time, and the energy analysis is inferred from the traffic and storage results, not modelled (pp. 7–8).
- **Contributions:** (1) An amortized argument that traffic per miss does not depend on core count when sharers are tracked exactly (p. 3, Fig. 2b p. 4). (2) Coherence traffic is at most 20% above a cache system without coherence (Table 1, p. 4). (3) With a hierarchy of inclusive caches, storage grows as √C (two levels) or ∛C (three levels) (p. 6, Fig. 4). (4) With explicit eviction notifications, recalls are rare: below 0.1% at a 4× capacity ratio with 8 ways (p. 7, Fig. 5). (5) Latency and energy arguments (pp. 7–8). (6) An extension to non-inclusive caches (p. 8).
- **Clarity:** Clear. There is one "Concern" section per scalability worry, and each ends with a boxed conclusion (pp. 3–8). There is a dedicated caveats section (pp. 8–9). One numeric inconsistency: the Introduction says storage is "2% of total cache size for even 512 cores" (p. 1); the Abstract gives no number. The summary says 5% with two cache levels or 2% with three (p. 8). The Introduction does not say that the 2% figure needs three levels.

## Concepts
| Concept | What the paper says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| new: single-writer, multiple-reader (SWMR) invariant | All coherence protocols ensure that, per block, there is either one core with write permission or any number with read permission. | p. 2 |
| new: scalable coherence | Coherence is "scalable" when its cost "grows (at most) slowly as core count increases". Five costs are examined: traffic, storage, inclusion, latency, energy. | p. 3 |
| new: amortized coherence traffic | A write that invalidates N sharers must follow N read misses, so average traffic per miss is constant in core count. | p. 3 |
| new: exact vs inexact encoding of sharers | Exact encoding uses one bit per core and gives constant traffic. Inexact (coarse-vector, limited-pointer) encoding bounds storage but causes "unnecessary invalidations", up to ~6× traffic at 1024 cores. | pp. 4–5 |
| new: hierarchical inclusive coherence | Cluster caches track their cores and the last-level cache tracks clusters. This gives fan-out invalidation, acknowledgment combining, and storage proportional to √C or ∛C. | pp. 5–6 |
| new: explicit eviction notifications | Private caches notify the shared cache on every eviction, including clean ones, so tracking is exact and recalls become rare. The cost is extra small messages. | p. 3, p. 6 |
| new: recall (back-invalidation) | When an inclusive shared cache evicts a block with sharers, it forces their eviction. This is rare (<0.1%) at a 4× capacity ratio. | p. 3, pp. 6–7 |
| new: direct vs indirect miss | Coherence adds latency only to indirect misses (blocks cached elsewhere), 1.5–2× a non-coherent miss, and the ratio does not depend on core count. | p. 7 |
| new: coherence messages implement shared memory | The coherent shared-memory abstraction is carried by point-to-point request, forward, invalidation, recall and ack messages between cache controllers. | pp. 2–3 |
| new: complexity shifting | Removing hardware coherence "does not remove the complexity, but rather shifts the complexity from hardware to software". | p. 9 |
| new: compatibility as reason for coherence | Coherence dominates for "technical and legacy reasons", and systems "tend not to abandon compatibility to eliminate small costs". | p. 1 |
| new: coexistence of communication paradigms | Message passing and scratchpads persist in HPC and real-time niches but "coexist with" coherent shared memory, which dominates mainstream chips. | p. 1, p. 8 |
