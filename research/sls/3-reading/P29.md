## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** Third-party measurement study (benchmark evaluation) of one processor against three contemporaries; a short report of 7 pages of text and tables plus 2 figure pages (pp. 17–26).
- **Context:** Intel's own claims for the 432 [Intel 81] and its project goals [Mazor 81] (p. 17); benchmark programs from EDN [Grappel and Hemmenway 81], Byte [Gilbreath 81], Baskett's puzzle and Wichmann's Ackermann study (p. 18; *Byte* is named only in the reference list, p. 24); procedure-call frequency studies [Clark and Levy 82], [Ditzel and McLellan 82], [McDaniel 82] (p. 18).
- **Correctness:** The premise is that an object-oriented architecture with "software-transparent multiprocessing and fault tolerance" may cost performance, posed as "An interesting question is how much performance is degraded" (p. 17). The authors hedge that "a high-level language system consists of the compiler and the machine, so we are not measuring just the architecture and hardware implementation" (p. 17). Four programs only, limited by "The time allotted to our visit at Intel" (p. 18). Several 1983 figures are forecasts, not measurements (p. 22).
- **Contributions:** Execution time and code size of four integer and character programs on the iAPX 432 (Ada, release 2 hardware and release 3 simulator) against the 8086, 68000 and VAX-11/780 (Pascal, C) (pp. 20–22). Headline: "A single 4 MHz, release 2 432 is currently about 1/20th of a VAX-11/780" (p. 23).
- **Clarity:** Clear and short; every machine configuration (clock, wait states, compiler version, who ran it) is listed (pp. 18–19). Weak points: the Table 2(a) header labels the search column "484" (p. 21); how the 432 release 2 times were normalised to 8 MHz in Table 3 is not explained (p. 22); the y-axis of Figure 2 is labelled only at 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5 (p. 26).

| Q | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | yes | The abstract states the aim: "an experiment to test the 432 as a high-level language uniprocessor" (p. 17). |
| Q2 | no | The paper describes no communication mechanism of the 432; it excludes "object-oriented programming, ... multiple processes, and multiple processors" from the study (p. 17). |
| Q3 | yes | Table 1 is "measured on real hardware", a release 2 432 system at Intel (pp. 19–20). The release 3 timings come from a simulator (p. 19), and the 1983 figures are forecasts (p. 22). |
| Q4 | partly | The measured results name their baselines (VAX-11/780 VMS Pascal, 8086, 68000 with clock, wait states and compiler; pp. 19–21). The 1983 claim ("1/5th the speed of Pascal programs on the 1983 VAX", p. 23) uses a hypothetical baseline. |
| Q5 | partly | Programs and the reason for each are given (p. 18) and the hardware configurations (pp. 18–19). The number of runs is not stated. |
| Q6 | partly | "about 1/20th of a VAX-11/780" matches Table 1(b) (.05–.07; p. 20). "1/5th" in 1983 rests on Figure 2 forecasts (p. 26). "5 processors may perform as well as a VAX" has no table or figure behind it (p. 23). The abstract has no quantitative claim. |
| Q7 | partly | The cost of parameter and global access is isolated by running modified programs (* rows, Table 1, p. 20). The code-size causes (no immediates, 16-bit addresses; p. 21) are only asserted. |
| Q8 | partly | Limits are mentioned in passing: compiler and machine measured together (p. 17), only four programs (p. 18), "we can not compare completely equal systems" (p. 23). There is no dedicated section. |
| Q9 | yes | All five authors are at UC Berkeley (p. 17), and none is named as a 432 designer. Intel's Konrad Lai ran the 32-bit release 2 and all release 3 runs (p. 19). |

## Notes
- **Source and version:** Internet Archive item `PerformanceEvaluationOfTheIntelAPX432` (uploaded 2019, scanned 2013 with a ScanSnap; the archive also offers an OCR text file). It is a 10-page photocopy whose printed page numbers 17–26 match the SIGARCH CAN 10(4) page range on Crossref. Page 17 carries a "THIS MATERIAL MAY BE PROTECTED BY COPYRIGHT LAW (TITLE 17 U.S. CODE)" stamp, so it is likely a library copy of the published issue (inferred). The paper is dated "May 17, 1982" (p. 17). The ACM full text (dl.acm.org) was blocked by a Cloudflare challenge, and Unpaywall and Semantic Scholar list no open copy. Saved as `papers/P29.pdf` (the image scan). Table values were read from the page images, not the OCR text, which garbles digits (8 → B).
- **Uploader's claim, not the paper's:** the Internet Archive description calls this "the paper that sank it" and says the 432 "quickly disappeared". This is not stated in the paper and is not used in any field above.
- **Criterion:** consistent with screening (I2; pp. 17, 20). The paper does not meet I1 on its own text: it never uses message, send, port, IPC or method invocation (checked over the full text). Whatever message mechanism the 432 has is outside this paper.
- **Scope:** uniprocessor, integer and character programs only. Object-oriented programming, floating point, multiple processes and multiple processors were excluded (p. 17), so none of the 432's object or inter-process mechanisms were measured.
- **Call frequency context:** "1 out of every 20 instructions executed is a procedure call or return" in other studies. Acker is "certainly on the high side with 1/7", and the others are low: puzzle 1/235, search 1/245, sieve 1/125000 (p. 18).
- **Paper errors:** the Table 2(a) column header reads "484" where the other tables say "search" (p. 21).
- **Procedure-call cost (moved from *Cost of message primitives* (gate 3c, 2026-10-12)):** Nearest proxy: Ackermann(3,6), "more than 170,000 procedure calls", "useful in measuring the cost of a procedure call", takes 260,000 ms on the 432 (4 MHz, release 2, Ada). That is at most about 1.5 ms per call including the work of the function (inferred). The 432 time for acker(3,6) was itself extrapolated from acker(1,2) by the VAX ratio. (pp. 18–20)
