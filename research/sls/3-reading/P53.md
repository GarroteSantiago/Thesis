## First pass: the five Cs
- **Category:** design description of a running commercial capability-based operating system kernel (KeyKOS nanokernel), plus implementation and a "limited performance comparison" (p. 14) of a UNIX built on it (KeyNIX) against Omron MACH 2.5 (p. 1, 2, 14).
- **Context:** microkernels; compares itself with Mach ports (p. 3), MACH 2.5 / MACH 3 and CHORUS (p. 13, 15, 17), Multics/80x86 ring security (p. 4); cites Hydra/C.mmp, Levy's capability systems, Lampson's confinement (p. 18).
- **Correctness:** the design claims rest on the authors' production experience ("in production use since 1983", p. 1); the performance claims rest on one small benchmark table with no stated number of runs and an explicit caveat: "A more careful analysis would be required for any serious evaluation" (p. 14). Several claims (transaction performance, >90% disk utilisation, task switch "several times lower than in competing microkernels") have no data in the paper (p. 8, 9, 13).
- **Contributions:** a kernel where "KeyKOS entities call upon the services of other entities by sending messages via a key" with "total reliance on capabilities without any other mechanisms" (p. 3); a stateless kernel with no dynamic allocation and no message buffering (p. 2, 7); keepers that turn exceptions into messages (p. 9); a binary-compatible UNIX with one UNIX keeper per process (p. 9-11) and its measured costs (p. 14-16).
- **Clarity:** clearly written; the kernel implementation itself is deliberately omitted ("we have omitted a description of the underlying kernel implementation", p. 2); measurement setup is thin.

## Concepts
| Concept | What the paper says | Page |
|---|---|---|
| new: key (capability) | "KeyKOS refers to capabilities as keys. Every object in the system is exclusively referred to by one or more associated keys"; holding a key is the authority to send to the entity. | p. 3 |
| new: key invocation | "Sending a message is sometimes referred to as key invocation"; call, fork and return operations. | p. 7 |
| new: resume key | Kernel-constructed key included in a call so the recipient can reply. | p. 3, 7 |
| new: stateless kernel | "hold no critical state in the kernel"; kernel private storage "is merely a cache of the persistent state"; no dynamic allocation. | p. 2 |
| new: single-level store | "all data lives in persistent virtual memory. Only the nanokernel is aware of the distinction between main memory and disk pages." | p. 3 |
| new: domain | Unit of program execution, "analogous to the virtual processors of the POSIX threads mechanism", with 16 key slots, address slot and meter key; single-threaded. | p. 6-7, 12 |
| new: keeper | "a domain that understands the exception messages delivered by the kernel"; domain, segment and meter keepers. | p. 9 |
| new: meter | Key conveying the right to execute for a unit of time; subdivided from a prime meter. | p. 6 |
| new: space bank | Domain managing disk resource allocation, subdivided from a master space bank. | p. 6 |
| new: factory | "an entity that constructs other domains"; clients can determine whether its products are secure. | p. 7 |
| new: principle of least privilege | Objects have "no intrinsic authority, and [rely] totally upon their keys". | p. 4 |

## Notes
- Source: full text, author manuscript PDF (18 pages, Word reformat, PDF dated 2007) from the MIT 6.858 (2014) readings page, found through the OpenAlex record W1604453322; saved as `papers/P53.pdf`. Venue and page range (USENIX Workshop on Micro-Kernels and Other Kernel Architectures, April 1992, pp. 95-112) taken from the HTML reprint at http://cap-lore.com/CapTheory/upenn/NanoKernel/NanoKernel.html (copyright notice by Jonathan S. Shapiro). Pages cited are PDF pages.
- Reference line discrepancy: the round file lists the first author as "Allen C. Bomberger"; the paper prints "Alan C. Bomberger" and lists seven authors (p. 1).
- Criterion: the full text confirms I1(c) (p. 3, 5, 7). No exclusion criterion applies: X2 does not (the messages are the kernel's own mechanism); X5 does not (18-page workshop paper). Whether it also meets I2 is doubtful: KeyKOS is called "a capability-based object-oriented operating system" (p. 1), not a computer architecture.
- The paper's numbers agree with each other: open/close "nearly four to one (3.89)" (p. 15) vs ratio 0.26 (p. 14); fork "a little more than six to one" vs 6.4.
- Concurrency (no criterion field applies): the paper claims "The absence of dynamic allocation means that there can be no interaction between dynamic allocation strategies, which is the predominant source of deadlock and consistency problems in most operating systems" (p. 2), with no data.
- KeyNIX mixes models: UNIX Keepers talk by messages but share process and open-file tables in a shared segment (p. 11), and the authors propose replacing this with a domain to permit distribution (p. 17).
- The paper is silent on message ordering and on how the 90-instruction and 500-cycle figures were obtained (p. 7, 13).
