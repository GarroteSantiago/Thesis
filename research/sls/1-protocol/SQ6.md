# SQ6

## Question
According to their designers and to later evaluations, why were language-directed, object-based and message-based machines abandoned, not adopted, or made unnecessary?

## Why it matters to the observation
The notes list earlier attempts (Intel 432, SOAR, J-Machine, Lisp machines) and hypothesise that the context that defeated them has changed (H1). The stated reasons for their decline are what that hypothesis must be checked against.

## Origin
notes

## Kind
evidence, because the question asks for the reasons stated for the decline of these machines: findings (stated reasons and who states them), not a list of machines. (`research/sls/1-protocol/step.md`, *How much each question needs*)

## Unit
A finding: a reason stated for a machine being abandoned, not adopted, or made unnecessary, with who states it (its designers or a later evaluation).

## Enough when
At least 10 papers serving it are read, in the reading order, then 5 in a row add nothing new. 10 + 5 rather than 10 + 3: each family of machines (capability processors, Lisp machines, Smalltalk processors, message-driven multicomputers) declined for its own reasons, so three papers on one family can add nothing while another family is still unread.

## Reading order
Machine families alternating, designers' retrospectives alternating with later evaluations, strongest first. Start with Ditzel and Patterson's retrospective on high-level-language machines (P280), the Intel 432 (P10, then P29), Lisp machines (P15), SOAR (P13), the compiler techniques that made special hardware unnecessary (P14, P60), message-driven machines (P33, P70), then the books (P1, P76, P72), the history of RISC (P84), the IBM Workplace OS experience (P189) and the ELXSI System 6400 (P42). Round 0 holds about 16 papers that serve it: the 13 included through I2 and the papers named above whose screening reason gives a reason for decline.
