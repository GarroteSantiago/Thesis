# SQ13

## Question
Which many-core processors were built without hardware shared memory or cache coherence, communicating between cores by messages or channels, how were they programmed, and why were they discontinued or not adopted?

## Why it matters to the observation
Hypothesis H3 says this design is different because it targets many cores with no shared state. That target has been built before: the Inmos Transputer with occam channels, and Intel's 48-core Single-chip Cloud Computer, a message-passing processor without cache coherence (Howard et al., 2010, ISSCC). If such chips were dropped for reasons that still apply, H3 does not separate this work from earlier failures.

## Origin
counter-evidence (added by skeptic)

## Kind
census, because the question asks why each such processor was discontinued or not adopted, and those reasons belong to each chip (its maker, its market, its programming model); a chip left out could be the one whose reason still applies to H3. The population is small, so covering every chip costs no more than saturating on kinds. (`research/sls/1-protocol/step.md`, *How much each question needs*)

Census or kinds is my decision at gate 1c; census is recommended. Estimate from `sls/2-search/R0.md`: 9 included papers name this question in their screening reason (P21, P70, P96, P97, P98, P100, P104, P175, P224); 7 more describe the same or similar processors (P12, P64, P65, P66, P99, P101, P176), counted from the processors their screening reasons name. Together they cover about 5 or 6 distinct processors: the Single-chip Cloud Computer, the Transputer, the Message-Driven Processor of the J-Machine, the M-Machine, the RAMP Blue emulation and the many-core of P101 (whether the M-Machine and P101 lack hardware shared memory is for the full text to say). Under census: about 8–12 papers from round 0 (one per processor, plus a second where the first does not say how it was programmed or why it ended), plus one per chip a later round finds. Under kinds: the stop rule needs at least 13 papers (10 + 3) for about 3 kinds, so it reads more, not fewer.

## Unit
One many-core processor built without hardware shared memory or cache coherence whose cores communicate by messages or channels, with how it was programmed and why it was discontinued or not adopted.

## Enough when
Every distinct processor is covered by one read paper, and a search round finds no new one. A processor counts as covered when the papers read for it say how it was programmed and why it was discontinued or not adopted, or when no paper found says so.

## Reading order
One processor at a time, alternating between decades, the paper that best covers programming and fate first: the Single-chip Cloud Computer (P21, then P104 for how it was programmed, P224 and P100 if still needed), the Transputer (P96, P99, P176), the Message-Driven Processor and J-Machine (P65, P70, P64, P12), the M-Machine (P66), RAMP Blue (P97), the processor of P101. Then the argument for message passing on many-core chips (P98) and PIKA (P175), for the reasons they give about such chips.
