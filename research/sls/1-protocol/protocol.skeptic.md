# Skeptic review of protocol

Saved next to the reviewed file as `protocol.skeptic.md`.

| Claim attacked | Attack | Evidence | Suggested fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| SQ1–SQ7 as a set (strongest form: "the seven questions map earlier attempts, their escape routes, interposition, coordination, performance, decline and defects, so the review covers the observation and the notes") | Bias toward support. Every SQ starts from the systems the observation favours (message-based ones) and asks how far they got. None asks whether the premises in notes.md are true: that the hardware model shapes programs, that closing the semantic gap is desirable, that messages and shared memory are not equivalent, that a fully self-defining system is possible, that interposition is absent below the network. A review run on SQ1–SQ7 can only report how far down messages went, never that going down is unnecessary or impossible. | Reasoning: each SQ1–SQ7 question names message-based, object-based or no-shared-state systems as its population; SQ7 is the only one that names a comparison. | Add SQ8–SQ19 (this row). In criteria.md, make sure papers that answer a counter-evidence SQ are included even if they never mention messages or objects. |
| SQ1: "Which computer systems provide sending a message ... as a primitive below the programming language ... and at which of these levels does each system's message semantics hold?" | Unanswerable as written: "message semantics hold" has no test, so two readers will place the same system at different levels. It also samples only on the outcome (systems that did it), so it cannot show how often attempts retreated from messages. | Reasoning. | Define in extraction-form.md one test for "holds at level L" (for example: every state change at L is caused by a message send, with no other path). Record systems that started message-based and later added shared-memory paths. |
| SQ2: "which mechanisms that are not message sends remain ... and which levels of each system are written in the system's own objects and messages?" | The strongest SQ in the set, but it treats every remaining mechanism as an escape route (a defect), without asking why designers kept it. If they were kept because they are needed, the observation's goal of "no escape routes" is the problem, not the systems. | Smith (1984), Reflection and Semantics in Lisp, POPL (reflective tower, at least virtually infinite levels); Ingalls et al. (1997), Back to the Future: The Story of Squeak, OOPSLA (virtual machine written in Smalltalk, translated to C to run). | Add to the extraction form a field "reason given for keeping it". Add SQ16 on the limits of self-definition. |
| SQ3: "Which message-based systems below the language level let a component ... be placed between a sender and a receiver without changing either" | Restricted to message-based systems, so it cannot test the observation's claim that conventional low-level machines do not allow interposition. That claim is contestable: virtualisation interposes a monitor between software and hardware. | Popek and Goldberg (1974), Formal Requirements for Virtualizable Third Generation Architectures, CACM 17(7) (conditions for a monitor to intercept every sensitive instruction). | Add SQ17 on interposition in conventional hardware and kernels, and ask in both SQ3 and SQ17 what interposition costs. |
| SQ4: "which of these functions still rely on a central coordinator?" | Phrased as if a remaining coordinator were a leftover to remove. It does not ask whether decentralised versions are possible at acceptable cost, nor about theoretical limits on agreement among message-passing parts. The notes' own analogy (networks) points the other way: networking moved to logically centralised control. | Fischer, Lynch, Paterson (1985), Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process, JACM 32(2); Kreutz et al. (2015), Software-Defined Networking: A Comprehensive Survey, Proceedings of the IEEE 103(1) (SDN promotes logical centralisation of control). | Add SQ15. In extraction, record coordination that is replicated or agreed upon (not only a single central node), since replication with agreement is still coordination. |
| SQ5: "to what causes do their authors attribute the differences?" | Authors' own attributions are the evidence most likely to favour the design (H2 rests on the 432's own evaluators, whose paper still names a residual 1–4 times slowdown as inherent to the 432's style, per notes.md citing [13, pp. 336–337]). No SQ asks for independent evaluations or separates inherent from implementation costs. | Hansen et al. (1982), A Performance Evaluation of the Intel iAPX 432 (independent evaluation; full citation not verified in this run); Woodruff et al. (2014), The CHERI Capability Model: Revisiting RISC in an Age of Risk, ISCA (modern capability hardware with measured overhead). | Add SQ18. In the extraction form, record for each performance figure who measured it (designers or third party) and whether the cause is classed inherent or implementation. |
| SQ5 and SQ6 (overlap) | Both will collect the 432, SOAR and J-Machine papers and both ask why they lost; SQ5 through measured causes, SQ6 through stated reasons. Fine to keep both, but the answers in row 5a may repeat each other. | Reasoning. | Keep both; in row 5a let SQ5 hold the numbers and SQ6 the non-performance reasons (market, compatibility, economics). |
| SQ6: "why were language-directed, object-based and message-based machines abandoned" | Looks only at the past, so it cannot test H1 ("the context changed"), which is a claim about the present. It also leaves out the no-shared-memory many-core chips that are the closest precedent for H3 (Transputer, Intel Single-chip Cloud Computer). | Howard et al. (2010), A 48-Core IA-32 Message-Passing Processor with DVFS in 45nm CMOS, ISSCC; transputer.net, Inmos and the Transputer (occam channels mapped to hardware links; decline after T9000 delays). | Add SQ13 (no-shared-memory many-cores) and SQ19 (post-Dennard specialisation). Make sure the search string catches "network-on-chip", "message-passing processor" and "transputer". |
| SQ7: "number and kinds of concurrency defects ... where each piece of state has one owner" | Neutral, and covers the Go counter-evidence; not duplicated here. Two risks remain. (1) notes.md declares deadlock, cross-actor atomicity and message-order effects out of scope or "intended semantics"; if extraction follows that framing, the defect classes actors actually have will be dropped. (2) It counts defects but not the escape from the model: programmers adding shared state to actor programs. | Torres Lopez et al. (2018), A Study of Concurrency Bugs and Advanced Development Support for Actor-based Programs (taxonomy of actor bugs, including message protocol violations and communication deadlocks); Tasharofi, Dinges, Johnson (2013), Why Do Scala Developers Mix the Actor Model with other Concurrency Models?, ECOOP. | Extraction for SQ7 records every defect kind, including those notes.md puts out of scope. Add SQ11 on mixing models. |
| notes.md premise: "The machine model shapes how programs are written [5][6]" | No SQ tests it, and its only support is two Turing Award lectures (essays). If languages and runtimes, not hardware, decide how programs are written, the motivation for changing the hardware weakens. | Reasoning (the two references are lectures, per seeds.md). | Add SQ8. |
| notes.md: "Semantic gap ... Goal: message semantics hold from the language down to the hardware" | No SQ asks whether closing the gap is desirable. The RISC argument was made against raising the hardware interface toward the language. | Patterson and Ditzel (1980), The Case for the Reduced Instruction Set Computer (argued against closing the semantic gap with richer instructions). | Add SQ9. |
| observation: message abstractions are "not in operating systems, hardware"; notes: actors "map onto many cores" | No SQ asks whether message passing and shared memory are equivalent, so that one can be layered on the other without loss. If they are, the gap is about which layer exposes which model, and a software layer may be enough. | Lauer and Needham (1978, reprinted Operating Systems Review 13(2), 1979), On the Duality of Operating System Structures. | Add SQ10 (duality) and SQ12 (limits of actor runtimes on conventional hardware). |
| notes.md H3: "shared memory has become a scaling cost [11]" | Rests on the Multikernel paper, whose authors argue for their own design. Contrary evidence exists that coherence and traditional kernels scale, and that multicore scaling as a whole is limited by power. | Martin, Hill, Sorin (2012), Why On-Chip Cache Coherence Is Here to Stay, CACM 55(7); Boyd-Wickizer et al. (2010), An Analysis of Linux Scalability to Many Cores, OSDI; Esmaeilzadeh et al. (2011), Dark Silicon and the End of Multicore Scaling, ISCA. | Add SQ14. |
| notes.md: "Networks work with protection and control at the endpoints [10]" read as "no central coordinator" | The end-to-end argument is about where to place a function, which is not the same claim as having no coordinator. This reading was not checked against the paper in this run, so it is raised as a question, not a finding. | Reasoning; Saltzer, Reed, Clark (1984) not read in this run. | Row 3a reader records what the paper actually claims about central functions; SQ15 collects the opposing evidence. |
| Unanswerable phrasing across SQ1, SQ2, SQ3 | "Below the programming language", "written in its own objects and messages" and "without changing either" need operational definitions, or inclusion will vary by reader. | Reasoning. | Define these three terms once in criteria.md or extraction-form.md before row 2a. |
| Count of new SQs (twelve) | Twelve counter-evidence SQs may be more than the review can answer well. Candidate merges: SQ13 with SQ14 (both attack H3), SQ9 with SQ19 (both about specialised hardware). | Reasoning. | Decision at row 1c: keep all, or merge the two pairs. |
