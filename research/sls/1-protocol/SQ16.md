# SQ16

## Question
What do studies of reflective and metacircular systems report about whether a system can be defined entirely in its own terms, what must remain outside it as a base (primitives, a level that is not reflected), and what that base costs or prevents?

## Why it matters to the observation
The observation says a system with escape routes "cannot define itself" and "cannot be taken seriously". Smith's reflective tower (1984, *Reflection and Semantics in Lisp*, POPL) is, at least virtually, an infinite stack of levels, and Squeak's virtual machine is written in Smalltalk but translated to C to run (Ingalls et al., 1997, OOPSLA). If every self-defining system needs a base that is not defined in itself, the gap may be unavoidable and the goal has to be restated as moving the base, not removing it.

## Origin
counter-evidence (added by skeptic)

## Kind
evidence, because the question asks what studies report about self-definition and the base it needs: findings (claims, constructions, limits). (`research/sls/1-protocol/step.md`, *How much each question needs*)

## Unit
A finding: a report of what a reflective or metacircular system could define in its own terms, what had to stay outside as a base, or what that base costs or prevents.

## Enough when
At least 10 papers serving it are read, in the reading order, then 3 in a row add nothing new.

## Reading order
Reflective towers (theory) and self-hosted implementations (practice) alternating, the founding papers first: Smith's tower (P26), Squeak (P27), the non-reflective account of the tower (P119), the metacircular virtual machine (P121), intensions and extensions in the tower (P120), the self-hosted JavaScript virtual machine (P128), Smalltalk-80's reflective facilities (P122), the Smalltalk-80 book (P16), then the other tower papers (P123, P124, P126, P127) and the metacircular Common Lisp special forms (P125).
