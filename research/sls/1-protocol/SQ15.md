# SQ15

## Question
Which system functions (resource allocation, scheduling, naming, protection, failure handling, global consistency) have been shown to require global agreement or to work better with central or logically central coordination, and what do decentralised versions cost or give up?

## Why it matters to the observation
The notes want to remove the middle layer entirely. Agreement among message-passing parts has proven limits (Fischer, Lynch and Paterson, 1985, *Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process*, JACM), and networking, the notes' own model, moved towards logically centralised control with software-defined networking (Kreutz et al., 2015, Proceedings of the IEEE). If key operating system functions need a coordinator, "no central coordinator" is a constraint that may make RQ5's answer "no" by construction.

## Origin
counter-evidence (added by skeptic)

## Kind
evidence, because the question asks which functions have been shown to need agreement or central coordination and what decentralised versions cost: findings (proofs, measurements, arguments), with the function named in each. (`research/sls/1-protocol/step.md`, *How much each question needs*)

## Unit
A finding: a proof, measurement or argument that a system function needs global agreement or works better with central or logically central coordination, or what a decentralised version of it costs or gives up.

## Enough when
At least 10 papers serving it are read, in the reading order, then 5 in a row add nothing new. 10 + 5 rather than 10 + 3: the evidence comes from distributed-computing theory, networking, cluster scheduling and operating systems, which use different models and measures.

## Reading order
Theory, systems measurements and arguments alternating, centralised and decentralised sides alternating: impossibility of consensus (P25), the cost of distributing logically centralised network state (P112), the end-to-end arguments (P7), hybrid centralised and distributed cluster scheduling (P115), the easy impossibility proofs (P114), distributed load balancing in switches (P117), the case for centralised network management (P116), hierarchical controllers (P118), auctions for decentralised scheduling (P113), the 1980s impossibility results revisited (P272), then the operating system papers (P46, P48, P70, P102). Round 0 holds 14 papers that serve it (the included rows whose screening reason names this question).
